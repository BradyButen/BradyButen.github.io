Project Overview

The turkeys declared war, so we built a robot dog to fight back

Wild turkeys on Michigan's campus have been attacking pedestrians, blocking traffic, and pecking at parked cars. They're federally protected, so you can't harm or relocate them. To address this problem, the Michigan Department of Natural Resources (DNR) sponsored us to find an autonomous solution to humanely deter turkeys away from high foot-traffic zones and into nearby wilderness areas.

Left: turkeys are blocking university public transportation. Right: turkeys are chasing a student just outside the mechanical engineering building.

The mechanical engineering marketing team filmed a full project breakdown near the end of the semester (I show up at around 2:17). Also, below is a high-level summary before diving into the technical details.

The build

We built a robot dog to patrol high foot-traffic zones around Michigan campus. The robot mimics dog behavior including walking, running, barking, and growling. A separate skid-steer drivetrain is in charge of maneuvering around rough, uneven outdoor terrain.

The brains

The autonomy stack runs computer vision to detect turkeys in the field of view, a UKF to fuse GPS and IMU data to estimate location in space, navigation algorithms to plan an obstacle-free pursuit path, and low-level controls to drive the robot's gestures and drivetrain. All of it runs in real-time on a Raspberry Pi 5.

The validation

We took our robot dog "Corndog" out near university campus and successfully scared live turkeys in manual mode using a custom operator UI. Autonomous features were validated separately against a static decoy in both indoor and outdoor environments.

Technical execution and task delegation done simultaneously

The project consisted of 13 students across three subteams. I served as technical lead of the controls & vision branch, taking on both a hands-on development role and broader team leadership. Below is an overview of my contributions across both fronts.

Technical dive: Navigation

Trajectory optimization and obstacle avoidance with MPC

The navigation stack runs in three layers. A* finds a globally optimal path through a precomputed occupancy grid, linear interpolation turns the discrete waypoints into a smooth reference trajectory at 10 Hz, and corridor MPC tracks it while enforcing hard speed and obstacle constraints in real time. MPC over simpler alternatives like PID or pure pursuit came down to one requirement: constraint enforcement. Speed limits near humans couldn't be reactive, and collision avoidance needed to be guaranteed by the solver, not hoped for at runtime.

A* Path Planning

→
Corridor MPC

A* with an EDT map where each cell is penalized based on distance to nearest obstacle. Open-space paths cost less than wall-hugging ones, so the planner routes with natural clearance.

→

A* outputs discrete waypoints so interpolation is added to produce a continuous reference trajectory at each \(\Delta t\) step, giving MPC a well-defined tracking target across the full prediction horizon.

→

MPC uses cost formulation to look \(N\) steps ahead and encode obstacle-free regions as soft linear constraints per timestep. Combined with a warm-start strategy, it runs at 10 Hz on a Pi 5.

Cost formulation

At each timestep, the solver finds the wheel speed sequence that minimizes tracking error and speed deviation while keeping the robot inside an obstacle-free corridor by construction. The full formulation runs at 10Hz on a Raspberry Pi 5 using CasADi/IPOPT with a 10-step prediction horizon.

$$J = \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} \Bigl[ (x_k - x_{r,k})^\top \textcolor{#2dd4bf}{\mathbf{Q}}\,(x_k - x_{r,k}) + (u_k - u_{r,k})^\top \textcolor{#2dd4bf}{\mathbf{R}}\,(u_k - u_{r,k}) \Bigr]$$
$$\begin{aligned} \text{where} \quad & x_k = [x,\; y,\; \theta]^\top \quad \text{(pose)} \\[4pt] & u_k = [v_L,\; v_R]^\top \quad \text{(wheel speeds)} \\[14pt] \text{s.t.} \quad & x_{k+1} = f(x_k,\,u_k) \\[6pt] & {-3} \leq v_L,\,v_R \leq 3\;\text{m/s} \\[6pt] & \textcolor{#2dd4bf}{\mathbf{A}}_k\,x_{k+1} \leq \textcolor{#2dd4bf}{\mathbf{b}}_k + \varepsilon, \quad k = 0,\ldots,N{-}1 \end{aligned}$$
Track position
\((x_k - x_{r,k})^\top \textcolor{#2dd4bf}{\mathbf{Q}}\,(x_k - x_{r,k})\)

Penalizes deviation from the A* reference path. Heading error is weighted more heavily to maintain visual contact with the turkey during active pursuit.

Match speed
\((u_k - u_{r,k})^\top \textcolor{#2dd4bf}{\mathbf{R}}\,(u_k - u_{r,k})\)

Penalizes deviation from reference wheel speeds. Weighted low to prioritize pursuit speed while still allowing deviations for smooth turning and fluid movement when needed.

Stay obstacle-free
\(\textcolor{#2dd4bf}{\mathbf{A}}_k\,x_{k+1} \leq \textcolor{#2dd4bf}{\mathbf{b}}_k + \varepsilon\)

Half-plane constraints keep every predicted state inside a convex corridor grown obstacle-free around the path. Slack \(\varepsilon\) prevents deadlock when transitioning between corridors.

Corridor construction

Each A* path segment generates one rectangular corridor: four half-plane constraints oriented perpendicular and along to the segment direction. The corridor expands outward until it hits an obstacle or reaches 0.5 m maximum half-width. Slack variables prevent deadlock in transition areas. This sidesteps the general MPC obstacle problem entirely. Instead of encoding every obstacle cell across the horizon, the solver sees only four linear inequalities per timestep, cheap to evaluate but also effective in preventing collisions.

Simulation results

Algorithmic performance was verified by selecting arbitrary start and end locations within the operating zone and plotting them in MATLAB. Visual inspection was used to determine whether the trajectories were satisfactory, with parameters tuned accordingly. This included adjusting corridor widths and time horizons to optimize for both fast response and robustness. Corridors that are too narrow risk potential deadlock, while corridors that are too wide may deviate too far from the reference path. Below are two plots showing the final algorithm in action.

The left plot shows the robot navigating from start (green) to end (red) while avoiding obstacles. The right plot is a close-up where the A* reference path, generated corridors, and MPC trajectory can be more easily observed.

Project Overview

A custom incubator built from scratch for Sartorius

Sartorius sponsored a custom live cell incubator to hold 37°C ±0.5 and above 90% relative humidity through long-duration cell culture runs, with no prior team's notes or known-good design to build from. Eight months, $13K, and nine vendors, from PCB fab down to McMaster and Adafruit for one-off parts. I led a six-person team and owned controls and software myself: the Raspberry Pi stack, the STM32 serial protocol, the operator interface, and a full firmware simulator.

Final enclosure: every sensor, actuator, and PCB spec'd, sourced, and integrated from scratch

My Role

Leading a six-person team while owning the software myself

I led a six-person team split into controls & software, embedded, and mechanical subteams: a real PCB needed its own embedded subteam, fast mechanical iteration needed its own, and I wanted to own software myself. A Gantt chart stayed in front of the team throughout and was the first signal when the mechanical subteam started falling behind. At close, I left a handoff document, walked the incoming cohort through the system, and wrote up recommendations for where to take it next.

On the technical side I owned the Raspberry Pi control stack, the firmware simulator, and the operator and researcher UIs. Control itself is deliberately simple: on-off switching against the setpoint with a hysteresis band tuned empirically on hardware, using the average of all ten sensor readings as the signal. It's the same approach production incubators and ovens use for slow thermal plants, where the real engineering is characterizing the plant well enough to pick that band. The STM32 protocol went through one rewrite: the first version used WebSocket-based JSON, which worked for me but was hard for Embedded to implement, so I switched to a fixed 5-byte format (command ID, device ID, and the command itself) that was much easier to build against.

Spending early

Prototyping and simulating the hardware before it existed

Before committing $13K and a full semester to real hardware, I built a $300 cardboard enclosure with off-the-shelf actuators to answer one question: could we control this environment at all? It told us what no simulation could: real thermal leakage behavior and workable sensor placement. It also exposed a flaw in the humidity actuator we'd planned around, ultrasonic atomizers with unreliable output and a wicking straw that kept drying out mid-run. I scrapped them mid-project for resistive heat pads, redesigned the mechanicals, and re-characterized the plant myself. The replacement reached setpoint 62% faster and is the reason we hit the humidity spec at all.

The testbed interior: off-the-shelf fans and heat pads, wired up to prove the environment could be controlled at all

The PCB was the other early risk I didn't want to wait on. I wrote a simulator that spoke the same Raspberry Pi to STM32 protocol over a virtual serial port, indistinguishable to the Pi stack from the real board. It didn't reimplement the STM32's firmware logic, just matched the message format and timing closely enough for the control stack, both UIs, and the automation layer to develop in parallel with hardware. A week building that stand-in bought roughly five months of parallel development; when the real board arrived, integration took hours.

User Interface

A wireless operator UI for live setpoint control and sensor data

Running an experiment originally meant sitting at a terminal watching numbers scroll. I built an operator UI for live setpoint control and real-time readings across all ten sensors, plus start and stop over the network, so an experiment didn't need someone watching it end to end. That made a 150-minute unattended run possible and let other experiments run entirely remotely, without my laptop in the loop. A researcher view and a device-mounted display share the same backend state.

The operator interface: live setpoint control and every sensor reading on one screen

What it came out to

Both specs held, though the enclosure leak was never fully solved

Sartorius specified a 37°C setpoint with ±0.5°C steady-state tolerance and a humidity floor above 90% RH for cell viability. We hit both, though not always as consistently as we wanted: the enclosure leaked, so temperature control was always fighting a thermal load different from the one on paper. We identified the leak but didn't have time to fully solve it, leaving it as a known issue for the next cohort. Humidity held once the heat pads reached setpoint 62% faster than the atomizers ever had.

Left: temperature settled within Sartorius spec and held. Right: heat pads reached setpoint 62% faster than the atomizer baseline and held above the 90% RH floor.

We closed one week ahead of an already-compressed deadline with a working prototype, full documentation, a validation dataset, and a walkthrough for the incoming cohort. The goal was never a perfect result in eight months, it was proving the environment could be built and controlled at all, and leaving the next team a real foundation to build on.

Learning to move quickly without moving carelessly

This project taught me to move quickly and stay a step ahead: testing for problems before they happened instead of reacting to them after the fact. That meant staying proactive with the team, honest about what wasn't working, and willing to move fast without cutting corners. I came out of it owning my work at a higher level, both technically and as a leader.

Project Overview

Aiming a beam of light at a set of fixed targets

Built for ME350 (Mechatronics), this was a five-person team project: design, build, and control a linkage mechanism to aim a beam of light at a series of fixed targets. Teams were scored on hits within a time cap, with weight and torque efficiency as tiebreakers. We hit every target and placed first. The sections below cover the mechanical design, the dynamics simulation that shaped it, and the control system that aimed it.

Mechanical design

Designing and optimizing the link geometry

Each of the five of us modeled a candidate linkage in SolidWorks, scored against nine weighted criteria from transmission angle deviation to manufacturability. Landing on a design took a lot of trial and error: sketching the transmission and follower links in 2D against the fixed target positions and iterating link lengths and pivot placement until the geometry held a good transmission angle without needing longer links to reach every target. My link lengths and placement were the ones that made it into the final design, integrated with the rest of assembly by the team.

Link positions to hit targets (R1-R5)
Final SolidWorks CAD assembly
Dynamic simulation

Validating motion in simulation before fabrication

Before cutting hardware, the full assembly went into MSC ADAMS for multibody simulation, shown at right. Driving it with a matching acceleration profile verified the motion path and produced torque and power curves at the input link, which we used to size the gear transmission and check every joint against failure with margin to spare. That simulation also set the 0.47 s move-time budget the motor and gearing had to hit.

Imported the SolidWorks model into ADAMS, then added links and joints to simulate kinematic and dynamic performance
Electronics & control

Closing the control loop from sensors to target

An Arduino Uno drives a DC motor through a gear transmission and H-bridge, while two IR sensors detect which targets are raised. A PID position loop then drives the flashlight to each target's calibrated encoder count and holds it there, with a limit switch homing the system at startup and a toggle switch arming the run.

Two fixes mattered most. The base logic defaulted to the nearest active target, which could send it oscillating between two that came up together, so we reprioritized to nearest-first and cut wasted travel. The rest came down to iterative PID tuning: adjusting gains, watching the response, and repeating until it braked in cleanly without overshoot.

Overview

A gesture-controlled wireless memory game

Built for EECS 373 (Embedded Systems) with one other teammate, this is a Simon-says-style memory game: one of six positions in a 3x2 LED grid lights up, the player repeats it by pointing, and each round adds one more position. It runs across a wearable hub and a camera-equipped base station linked by a wireless protocol I designed, and I owned the system end to end. The full loop ran live end to end: sequence display, gesture tracking, and scoring. The sections below cover the wireless link and sensor fusion, the camera calibration, and the game logic driving it.

Wearable node & wireless link

Reconciling two hand sensors into one shared frame

The wearable is a single STM32 worn on the body, reading two hand-mounted accelerometers and driving a haptic buzzer that confirms a correct gesture. It talks wirelessly to a second STM32 at the base station. XBee only gives you wireless UART, so I designed the protocol on top of it myself, deciding what every byte meant. Since the two accelerometers sit at different orientations, raw readings get reconciled into a shared frame before each gesture is classified and sent across.

Both gloves worn mid-round, streaming gesture data to the base station
Haptic buzzer test rig, tuning vibration feedback for the glove
Perception & calibration

Calibrating the camera to track a gesture

The IR camera was mounted at an unknown orientation and could be mirrored relative to the grid, so there was no fixed pixel-to-space mapping to hard-code in advance. Calibration walks the player through all four corners in order, top-left to top-right to bottom-right to bottom-left, holding each point until the IR signal reads clean and stable for three seconds before it's averaged and accepted. From those four correspondences, I compute their eigenvectors and build a new basis that maps any later camera reading onto grid coordinates, correcting for both rotation and reflection in how the camera sits.

Calibration diagram: the camera's rotated, mirrored view corrected into grid space
Game logic & peripherals

Driving the game logic and its outputs

The base station runs the entire game as a finite state machine I designed, moving through idle, sequence display, gesture validation, and round advancement as the player progresses. From there, it drives everything the player actually sees and feels: the LED grid lighting up the sequence, an LCD screen running on a driver I wrote from scratch, haptic feedback buzzing the glove to confirm a hit, and the camera watching for each new gesture. Underneath all of that, a companion laptop app stays connected over a serial link, handling audio cues and keeping score between rounds.

Overview

Building error-proofing systems in Factory Zero's paint shop

I interned on the paint shop controls team, working two separate vision-based error-proofing problems: cutting faults on the plant's automated body-plug line, and building a camera system to stop one of six visually similar charge port door variants from getting installed on the wrong vehicle.

Factory Zero production floor
Plug Cell

Robots install 12,000 plugs a day, so I automated inspection

FANUC robots insert roughly 12,000 plugs a day, each verified by IR machine vision. On a sampled day, the line logged 524 false-positive faults against just 37 real ones. Memory limitations meant the cameras kept only the pass/fail measurements, not the images behind them. So I built an OCR logger that taps into the live camera feed on the plant floor, reads the results directly, and saves the images to my computer, bypassing the old system entirely and letting me verify plugs visually even when I wasn't on the floor.

What the camera sees
Pass: plug detected and confirmed
Fail: a false-positive fault, no plug missing

The tool gave me a UI for defining scanning zones per camera, OCR to classify plug type and pass/fail result, and automatic image capture, paired with an Excel model tracking all 272 unique plug styles and their degrade scenarios. That turned a vague sense that faults were high into a precise, per-style record of exactly where they were coming from.

That data drove an iterative loop: tune a threshold or reteach a robot, watch the fault data shift, then go verify by hand on specific cars further down the line where the numbers looked off. Plugs typically sit low on the vehicle underbody, and you'd need to be 10 feet tall to see them, so I had to be deliberate about what I could verify directly and lean on operators on the floor to help confirm the rest. Daily vision faults dropped from over 400 to about 25. The overall fault rate went from 4.1% to 0.58%, with the worst-offending style, ZL, falling from 18.6% to roughly 1%.

Charge Port Doors

A camera check to stop the wrong door from getting installed

Six charge port door variants run through one shared install footprint, and pairs like CC/CG and YL/ZL look nearly identical but aren't interchangeable. The existing pick lights couldn't catch mixed supplier stock, so a wrong door wouldn't surface until General Assembly, which became a two-shift scramble to fabricate a replacement. I evaluated a mobile hand scanner against a fixed-mounted camera, and walked the decision through with the operator at that station: we talked through the pros and cons of each, ran through the actual door-handling motion together, and felt out where a camera could realistically sit. A handheld scanner would've been messy with sealer on the operator's hands, and its drop-off point was too far from the work area, so the camera won out. Finding the right spot mattered as much as the technical choice, since if the operator didn't like the system, they'd find a way around it.

Once we settled on a camera and its placement, I trained the camera's OCR to read each door's style character, tuned lighting, positioning, and trigger speed. I also integrated the PLC logic that switches the camera's active program and reads back its result automatically. On the HMI side I added new faults and alarms, a bypass button, and a stack light for operator status. After a few rounds of feedback with the operator on the floor, I authored the Task Instruction Sheet for validating the whole system end to end, and worked with Industrial Engineering on the accompanying Job Element Sheet, the standardized work the line runs on now. The finished system added about a second to operator cycle time and is expected to eliminate the mis-install problem entirely, pending final validation.

From my time in the Paint Shop, I learned that it doesn't matter how well a system is built if the people using it don't trust it. Earning that trust turned out to be the harder problem, spanning everything from making sure the system behaved correctly under edge cases and was properly tuned, to making sure the right documentation and training existed. Without alignment between operators and the technology, overrides get given without a second thought, and defects can spiral into costs of thousands for the company. Adopting a listening-first mindset made me a better engineer, one who builds real relationships with people to make sure not just that a problem gets solved, but that the right problem is understood and solved.

Overview

Engine Cooling Fan

A pneumatic fan needed a robust control loop

The engine cooling fan's blade pitch is set by a pneumatic valve, and how it was being driven caused real problems: too little actuation ran the engine hot, wide open all the time was needlessly loud, and constant oscillation wore the mechanism down faster than it needed to. I needed a control loop that could hold a stable pitch across real field conditions, with fan reversing, the AC compressor, and engine cooling demand all shifting the target at once.

The cooling fan, installed in the engine bay
Bench test rig: fan, controller, and logging laptop

Existing tools gave almost no visibility into what the valve was doing, so I connected to the SPU70 over CAN and built a bench setup that logged every signal in real time. From there I wrote a per-unit calibration routine, an auto-control mode with tuned on-off pulses, and fault detection for the failure modes that mattered, then ran two weeks of step and ramp tests before the first engine test.

Actual pitch (blue) tracking setpoint (red) under changing step-inputs

I tried a simple on-off controller first, but it was unstable, so I reworked the loop to add deliberate delays, driving the valve in tuned on-off pulses instead of chasing the setpoint continuously. That dropped average tracking error under aggressive ramp and step changes from 11.8° to 2.9°. From there it went onto the machine itself, where it held up through a dealer meeting, a tuning visit from Allison Transmission, and the usual debugging moves elsewhere on the machine, logging over 15 hours of real drive time so far. Faults behaved correctly throughout, and with only minor tweaks since, it's still running on the machine today.

Park Brake Redundancy

Building a safety-critical system that people can lean on

The park brake is effectively our emergency stop, so this couldn't just work most of the time, it had to be precise about every single edge case. Its status comes from three independent sources: a hardwired SPU70 line and two redundant CAN channels, ChA and ChB. Any one of them can fail, read wrong, or time out on its own, and the system has to know exactly when to trust which signal, because getting that wrong in either direction is dangerous: miss a real failure and the brake doesn't hold when it should, or trip the fallback incorrectly and it could engage while someone's driving at speed.

I wrote the specification that decided exactly how to handle every one of those cases: what happens when the hardwire disagrees with the CAN channels, when one channel times out but the other doesn't, when both go dark at once. Every combination maps to a specific behavior, normal operation, a minor fault, or a major fault that engages the brake as soon as it's safe to.

Every signal combination mapped to a fault classification and behavior

Most of the real thinking went into the electrical failure modes, not just the software logic: a wire can break, short to ground or power, or report a value that's technically in range but physically impossible, and each case needs to be diagnosed differently. I implemented that classification directly on the SPU70, reading the raw CAN bytes off both channels and the hardwired line every cycle and stepping through the mismatch and timeout logic in real time. The 1.5 mph speed lockout ties into the same thinking: a major fault means the machine shouldn't keep driving on safety logic it can no longer fully trust, so it locks out below walking speed and forces a service call instead.

Autosteer Disengage

Turning the wheel is the only way out of autosteer

Our autosteer disengage works off a steering sensor: turn the wheel and the system drops out of autosteer. This had to be reliable, since a noisy signal could get the setup misread as an encoder, and there's no other way to disengage autosteer if that logic fails, so it carried the same weight as any other safety-critical system on the machine.

I wrote the new SPU70 code that implements the disengage and validated it on a 1:1 mapping of the actual setup: two controllers, the same steering sensor, and an oscilloscope to see the real output values. I also built a UI during testing to fine-tune the trigger lengths by feel.

Vehicle Lighting

Lighting isn't just cosmetic when you're spraying at night

Lighting matters more than it might seem: night spraying happens in the pitch dark, and if the lights aren't responsive, adjustable, and just working, the operator loses real visibility out in the field. I wired up control for the full set: work lights, hood lights, beacon, light bars, mood/dome/marker lights, hazards, and the keypad, shuffle, and shifter backlighting.

Beyond just switching lights on and off, I integrated the system with the display for dimming and a day/night mode, added backlighting to the keypad and its function buttons, and built steering column macros so specific lights can be triggered with a single button override instead of digging through menus. I also debugged the hazards after they weren't behaving correctly, and handled the hood lights and work lights themselves. On top of that, the redesigned steering column stalk added dedicated park/road/field modes, plus a configurable automatic shutoff so nothing gets left on by accident.
